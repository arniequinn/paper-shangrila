Why do empires rise, fall, and rise again?

Paper Shangri-La is a short, personal tour through the history that shaped the Middle East and South Asia, read through Ibn Khaldun, the 14th-century thinker who believed every civilization follows a cycle of growth and decline.

It follows the family tree shared by the Bible and the Quran, the Prophet's world and the succession crisis, the Islamic Golden Age, Genghis Khan's destruction at Otrar, the Mughal prince Dara Shikoh, and the Partition of 1947 and the war of 1971. It then turns to the Anthropocene, hostile architecture, and artificial intelligence, and asks what they say about our pursuit of utopia.

Its argument is that no society reaches a perfect world, only a better direction. The danger comes when a virtue such as power, liberty, or knowledge is cut off from the one that completes it.

The book closes with a personal epilogue and a small collection of original poems.

About the author

Tokumei Daredemo is a pen name, roughly "anonymous, anyone." The author works in the design, documentation, and visualization of architecture, and has long been fascinated by the built and natural environments and the way each shapes the other.

Paper Shangri-La grew out of an earlier, unpublished collection of poems, Vessels of Light, and a long effort to verify the early history of Islam and the world around it.
